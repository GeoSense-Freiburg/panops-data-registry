OVERVIEW

About this report

Two datasets, both restricted to sites with mean tree cover ≥ 30 % within 500 m, and three learners, all tuned with Optuna (TPE, 50 trials, grouped 5-fold site CV). Switch between them at the top; every figure and the metrics table follow the switches.

Naming. V2.2 is the analysis previously called V10 (deadtrees v2-2 maps, 93 sites); V4.1 is the new paper dataset (deadtrees v4.1 maps). Folder and file names still use V10.

V4.1 Flux is the V4.1 EFP with the four EFPs replaced by growing-season totals from the half-hourly FLUXNET data: GPP and NEP (g C m−2), ET (mm) and WUE = GPP/ET (g C mm−1), summed over the same growing-season days as the EFPs. A seasonal sum is deflated by missing data, so site-years with < 80 % of the growing season present are dropped (26 of 388; CA-LU1, US-GLE, US-NC3 and US-SHC lose all years).

V2.2 Flux uses the V2.2 predictors (deadtrees v2-2, V2.2 climate lags, traits and 11-family mortality block; 93 sites) with exactly the same flux totals, completeness gate (23 of 395 site-years dropped, no site lost), tuning, repeated CV, SHAP and figures as the V4.1 flux set, so the two flux sets differ only in their predictors and site-years. It replaces the earlier predict_fluxes/ run (daily files, GPPsat tuning reused, no SHAP), whose results are kept in that folder.

With either V4.1 dataset or the V2.2 Flux selected, RF Optuna and Leave-one-site-out are greyed out because they were not run for it. Sections without a V4.1 counterpart say so. What differs between the two datasets beyond the mortality maps (climate window, NEPmax definition, traits, years) is listed in Section 11, together with a direct comparison of the results.

Cross-validation structures

Every result is available under two resampling structures, switchable at the top left:

Leave-one-site-out (LOSO)

Each site is held out in turn and predicted by a model trained on all remaining sites. Tests extrapolation to an unseen site. Primary structure.

Repeated LOSO-CV (3 × 80%)

Each held-out site is predicted three times, each from a different random 80 % subsample of the remaining sites; predictions are averaged. Tests whether a result depends on one particular split, and yields a per-prediction stability measure (pred SD in the metrics table).

The two agree on every conclusion — model-rank agreement ρ = 0.85–0.96, disturbance effect correlated at r = 0.966, same best learner in all six dataset × CV combinations. Repeated CV is slightly more optimistic (it averages three predictions) and has less statistical power, so LOSO is used as the primary structure and repeated CV as a robustness check. See Section 13.

Disturbance metrics

For visualisation and thresholding we define three disturbance metrics, all computed within a 500 m buffer around each tower from the deadwood / tree-cover maps:

Absolute mortality

Standing deadwood cover as a percentage of the total buffer area — the absolute amount of dead canopy.

absolute mortality = deadwood / area × 100 (%)

Relative mortality

Deadwood relative to tree cover — what % of the standing trees are currently dead (defined where tree cover ≥ 10 %, capped at 100 %).

relative mortality = deadwood / tree cover × 100 (%)

Relative disturbance

Combines standing deadwood and tree-cover loss relative to forest plus loss — captures both dead-standing and removed/lost canopy.

relative disturbance = (deadwood + loss×100) / (forest + loss×100) × 100 (%)

Models

Predictor blocks: C climate, T plant traits, M memory (the response's own previous-year value, V2.2 only), D disturbance / mortality metrics. Pairs that differ only by D isolate the effect of adding disturbance. Every model runs at a 12-month and a 24-month lag window. The V4.1 EFP uses only M3 and M4.

Disturbance-level thresholds

To classify each site's disturbance level into Low / Mid / High for each metric, we compare three thresholding schemes (all computed on the per-site peak value):

Tertiles

Equal-sized groups split at the 33rd and 67th percentiles — each class holds ~⅓ of sites (balanced statistical power, ignores natural clustering).

Equal-width

The min-to-max range divided into three equal-width bins — reflects the distribution shape but yields unequal group sizes (most sites fall in the low bin).

Natural breaks

Cut points at mean ± 0.5·SD — Low below, High above, Mid in between; grounded in the variance structure and roughly balanced.

Model performance & effect of disturbance on RMSE

Per-site RMSE without disturbance (cyan, M3 = climate + traits) and with it (pink, M4 = + mortality), for XGBoost and LightGBM (Optuna) at the 12 m and 24 m windows, repeated CV (3 × 80 %). The same figure for all four datasets; it does not follow the learner and CV switches.

Reading the labels. Each pair carries three numbers: % the median paired change in per-site RMSE (negative = less error, green); stars a one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, H1: adding D reduces per-site RMSE, with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR across the 16 tests in the figure (*** q<0.001, ** q<0.01, * q<0.05, ns); and %↓ the share of sites whose RMSE improved. Sites are paired on SITE_ID. Statistics are written to fig2_statistics.csv (or fig2_high_*_statistics.csv) beside each figure.

Caveat. The effect size is the median of the paired per-site changes, not the difference of the two medians. Because the models share training sites, the per-site errors are not fully independent, which makes p-values mildly anti-conservative; strong results are safe, marginal ones (q ≈ 0.05) should not be over-read.

High-disturbance sites only. The same figure and statistics restricted to the sites whose maximum disturbance across years lies above the natural break (mean + 0.5 SD over sites; the number of sites is given in the figure subtitle). Models are trained on all sites; only the sites shown and tested change. With ~20–26 sites the tests have less power, so read the effect size together with the stars.

  • Same pattern in all four sets. Mortality improves the carbon fluxes and WUE and not ET, in V2.2 and V4.1, for EFPs and for totals. The ET null holds everywhere except the V4.1 ETmax.
  • V2.2 vs V4.1 totals. Mortality adds more in V2.2: GPP R² 0.27–0.31 → 0.40–0.43 (per site −6 to −10 %, all significant) and NEP −5 to −7 % (all significant), versus GPP 0.55–0.57 → 0.57–0.59 in V4.1. The difference sits in the baseline: with the same responses and tuning, V4.1 climate + traits explain GPP far better (R² ≈ 0.56 vs 0.29) and NEP too (≈ 0.24 vs ≈ 0), leaving less for mortality. What makes the V4.1 baseline stronger (pre-season climate window, Lusk traits, site set) has not been isolated.
  • Windows. 24 m beats 12 m for the V4.1 fluxes (GPP and WUE significant mainly at 24 m); for the V2.2 fluxes and the EFPs the two windows are about equal.
  • High vs Low+Mid SHAP (Fig. 4): GPP and WUE significant in all 8 tests in both flux sets; NEP in 7 of 8 (V2.2) and 4 of 8 (V4.1, relative disturbance only); ET never.
  • Caveat: the V4.1 completeness gate removes all years of two strongly disturbed sites (CA-LU1, US-GLE) and some of DE-Akm and US-NC2, so the V4.1 flux set carries less high-disturbance data.

Reading the comparison

  • Same direction, smaller increment. Mortality still helps GPPsat, NEPmax and WUE, and NEPmax is still the strongest. M4 is as good as or better than in V2.2, but the M3 baseline is much stronger (GPPsat ≈ 0.18 → 0.33, NEPmax 0.18 → 0.42, WUE 0.12 → 0.43), so the R² gain from mortality is about a third to a half of V2.2's (a quarter for WUE).
  • The stronger baseline is mostly the site set. On the 303 shared site-years M3 is not stronger in V4.1 (GPPsat 0.16–0.20 vs 0.12–0.16; WUE −0.23 to −0.06 vs 0.04–0.09). The 23 new sites and years widen the range of WUE between sites, which traits capture. On the shared site-years mortality still adds +0.07 to +0.09 R² (GPPsat) and +0.07 to +0.27 (WUE). R² depends on the spread of the sample, so compare the datasets on RMSE.
  • ETmax moved from a slight loss to a slight gain (≈ +0.02 R²). It holds on the 83 shared sites (median −1 to −4 % per site vs +1 to +2 % in V2.2) and is larger at the 23 new sites (−6 to −12 %). M3 skill for ETmax is unchanged, so the change is in what mortality adds; it is concentrated in ENF and MF, not DBF.
  • Not explained by the date fix. The per-site change in gain is unrelated to how far V4.1 moved the map date (Spearman ρ between −0.15 and 0.12, all p > 0.17), and DBF sites, with the largest shifts, show no ETmax change. The corner fix, masking, lag structure, traits, years and tuning also changed; a swap test (V4.1 pipeline with the v2-2 mortality block) would isolate the maps.

2 · Does disturbance matter more for forests? No evidence

Tested at site level (one value per site, pooled over all 9 learners, so no pseudo-replication), on all_sites which spans the full gradient:

  • Tree cover vs disturbance benefit: Spearman ρ = 0.044, p = 0.65 — no relationship
  • Forest (83 sites) −3.09% vs non-forest (29 sites) −7.09%: p = 0.163 — not significant, and pointing opposite to the hypothesis

Caution: pooling all site × pair × EFP rows gives "non-forest benefits more, p = 1e-08". That is pseudo-replication — 60 non-independent rows per site — and the effect disappears once aggregated per site. Do not quote the pooled version.

Conclusion: the disturbance effect is EFP-specific, not ecosystem-specific. It depends on which function is predicted (carbon yes, water no), not on ecosystem type.

4 · Learner — why not the most accurate one

RF_exp02 has the highest mean R² (0.372 vs XGB_optuna 0.358) but was rejected. Its always.split.variables = raw memory hard-codes a structural privilege for one predictor block. RF_exp02 and RF_base are identical without raw memory (0.234 both); the forcing is worth +0.244 R² there and nothing elsewhere.

The defence that exists: memory is the control (an autoregressive nuisance term), disturbance is the treatment; forcing the control is conservative — RF_exp02 shows the weakest disturbance effect of the RF family. Forcing disturbance instead would be circular.

But the forcing is unnecessary. RF_optuna reaches essentially the same raw-memory performance (0.644 vs 0.648) with no forcing — purely by tuning mtry to 0.72–0.91 of predictors instead of ranger's sqrt(p) default (~0.05 when p ≈ 449). With ~21 of 449 predictors sampled per split, RF picked the memory variable by chance ~5% of the time. The forcing was a workaround for a hyperparameter pathology, not a scientific statement.

XGB_optuna selected: highest R² of any non-forcing learner, hyperparameters from a defined TPE search space, disturbance effect mid-pack (−2.15%, 78.3% improved) so not cherry-picked, and disturbance SHAP share 26.7% — slightly above RF_exp02's 25.9%.

Caveat: RF_base has the highest disturbance SHAP share (35.6%) partly because it is the worst model — its raw-memory models underperform (0.404), so proportionally more attribution falls to disturbance. Not evidence that disturbance matters more.

5 · Six things to state explicitly in the manuscript

  1. Justify tc≥30 a priori — "the forest-relevant subset" — not because it showed the strongest effect. The other two datasets are in the appendix showing the same direction.
  2. The 2 raw-memory significant results are fragile. GPPsat 12m and WUE 24m (* under LOSO) drop to 0/10 under repeated CV. The defensible line: once raw EFP memory is included, disturbance adds no consistent improvement.
  3. ETmax is a consistent null — ns in every configuration, slightly positive in places. An honest null across 3 datasets × 9 learners × 2 CV structures is a result; report it.
  4. uWUE/WUE are borderline — significant at 24m, not 12m. Describe as weak and window-dependent.
  5. Do not compare R² across datasets. tc≥50's R² is depressed ~41% by range restriction (GPPsat sd 8.72 → 6.97) while its RMSE improves ~6.7%. Use RMSE across datasets; R² only for ranking models within one.
  6. Robustness sentence available: "The disturbance effect is invariant across nine learner configurations (RF, XGBoost, LightGBM, each with tuned and Optuna variants) and two resampling structures; the adopted configuration yields a mid-range estimate."
