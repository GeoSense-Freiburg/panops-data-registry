\section*{Abstract}
{\small\textit{Draft for Nature Ecology \& Evolution: unreferenced, $\leq$200 words.}}

Tree mortality is increasing in forests worldwide, yet how it changes ecosystem functioning across sites remains
poorly quantified. Here we combine eddy-covariance measurements from 101 sites with at least 30\,\% tree cover (361 site-years;
forests, woody savannas, shrublands and wetlands) with
remote-sensing maps of tree mortality and machine learning, and show that knowing about mortality makes their carbon
uptake markedly more predictable. Adding mortality to climate and plant traits reduced the prediction error of maximum
net ecosystem productivity by 11--12\,\% overall and by about 30\,\% in years with high mortality, and improved
water-use efficiency mainly where mortality occurred; maximum evapotranspiration did not improve. Without mortality,
models over-estimated carbon uptake and water-use efficiency in high-mortality years, a bias that grew with the amount of
mortality and that mortality information reduced by about two-thirds. The direction of the effect followed the amount of
mortality rather than vegetation type or tree cover, and the results held when whole sites were resampled and
when the climate variables used to calculate each property were left out. Mortality maps can thus correct a systematic over-estimate of
carbon uptake in woody ecosystems with high mortality.

\section*{Main}
    \begin{itemize}
    \item Terrestrial ecosystems regulate global \textbf{carbon, water, and energy balances}; forest functioning is closely linked to vegetation structure and functional traits.

    \item Increasing \textbf{drought, heat, and biotic disturbances} are causing widespread tree mortality, with potentially large consequences for ecosystem functioning.

    \item Mortality alters \textbf{canopy structure and leaf area}, affecting carbon uptake, evapotranspiration, and energy partitioning, with cascading effects on soils, nutrients, and vegetation recovery.

    \item The magnitude and trajectory of these impacts likely depend on \textbf{vegetation type and functional trait composition}.

    \item However, quantitative understanding of mortality impacts across ecosystems remains limited due to \textbf{spatial and temporal constraints of field observations} and limited integration of mortality and trait data.

    \item Advances in \textbf{remote sensing, machine learning, citizen science, and trait databases} now enable large-scale assessments of mortality and ecosystem responses.

    \item This creates an opportunity to quantify how mortality affects ecosystem functioning and identify \textbf{vegetation types that are more sensitive or resilient}.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Mortality across 101 woody ecosystems}
We combined four ecosystem functional properties --- light-saturated gross primary productivity (GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$), maximum net
ecosystem productivity (NEP$_\mathrm{max}$), maximum evapotranspiration (ET$_\mathrm{max}$) and water-use efficiency (WUE) --- from 101
eddy-covariance sites with at least 30\,\% tree cover (361 site-years) with annual remote-sensing maps of standing
deadwood and tree cover (Fig.~1). Most sites are temperate and boreal forests (49 evergreen needleleaf, 30 deciduous
broadleaf and 7 mixed forests); the others are wetlands (6), evergreen broadleaf forests (3), shrublands (5) and a woody
savanna. We grouped the sites by their maximum relative mortality, the share of the tree cover that died or was lost in a
year: Low ($<$\,10.6\,\%, 41 sites), Mid (38 sites) and High ($>$\,24.0\,\%, 22 sites). For each property we trained
machine-learning models on climate and plant traits (C+T) and on climate, traits and mortality (C+T+M) and evaluated
them with leave-one-site-out cross-validation, so that every prediction is made for a site the model has not seen
(AutoGluon, an automated ensemble of machine-learning models).

\subsection*{Mortality improves predictions}
To test whether knowing about mortality makes ecosystem function more predictable, we compared the errors of the two
models. Adding mortality raised the mean cross-validated $R^2$ from 0.35 to 0.40 with the 12-month predictor window and
from 0.35 to 0.41 with the 24-month window (Table~\ref{tab:r2} and Fig.~2a). The gain was largest for NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ ($+0.11$), followed by WUE
($+0.06$) and GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$ ($+0.04$ to $+0.05$); the gain was significant for NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ and WUE in both windows and for
GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$ at 24 months (paired permutation test with whole sites swapped, $q<0.05$), whereas ET$_\mathrm{max}$ did not improve. With every site-year as an observation and whole
sites resampled, mortality reduced the mean absolute error (MAE) of NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ by 11.8\,\% (95\,\% bootstrap interval
6.3--17.0\,\%) and 10.8\,\% (5.1--16.0\,\%) for the 12- and 24-month windows (Table~\ref{tab:main} and Fig.~2b). The reduction was
largest in the years in which trees died: in site-years with more than 24\,\% relative mortality the NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ error fell by
31.6\,\% (16.0--43.2\,\%) and 29.2\,\% (8.6--42.9\,\%), and the WUE error by 16.9\,\% (5.1--25.2\,\%) and
21.8\,\% (8.2--34.5\,\%), about four times the reduction across all site-years ($-4.3$ and $-4.7$\,\%).
GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$ improved less ($-4$ to $-12$\,\%; significant across all site-years and in the years with more than 10.6\,\%
mortality), and ET$_\mathrm{max}$ improved only slightly ($-3.9$ and $-5.4$\,\% in the years with more than 10.6\,\% mortality).
Mortality information therefore matters most for the carbon fluxes and WUE, and most in the years in which trees died.

\subsection*{Without mortality, models over-predict}
To find where these errors come from, we grouped the residuals (observed $-$ predicted) by the relative mortality of
each site-year (Fig.~3). Without mortality, the model predicted too high in high-mortality years. In site-years with more
than 30\,\% relative mortality (21 site-years at 10 sites) it over-predicted NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ by 4.0\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$ (95\,\% interval
2.4--5.5), GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$ by 4.3\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$ (1.3--7.5) and WUE by 0.77\,g\,C\,mm$^{-1}$ (0.29--1.10), whereas the residuals were close to zero in years
with little mortality. Adding mortality reduced these over-predictions by about two-thirds, to 1.3, 1.1 and 0.43, with
intervals that included zero. This correction --- the amount by which adding mortality lowered the prediction --- was
significant in the $>$\,30\,\% class (GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$ $+3.2$, NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ $+2.7$\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$, WUE $+0.34$\,g\,C\,mm$^{-1}$; all $q<0.01$, paired permutation test
with whole sites swapped) and grew with relative mortality (mixed model with a random intercept per site, per
$+10$\,\% relative mortality: $+0.96$, $+0.75$ and $+0.10$; all $q<0.01$, site-block bootstrap; Fig.~A12). In years with less than 10\,\% mortality the
mortality predictors instead raised the predictions slightly. ET$_\mathrm{max}$ showed no over-prediction without mortality; with mortality its residual rose slightly with relative mortality ($+0.006$\,mm\,d$^{-1}$ per $+10$\,\%, $q<0.05$; Fig.~A12), a small over-correction that did not increase its error (Table~\ref{tab:main}). Across all 361 site-years, the
residual of the model without mortality declined with relative mortality (per $+10$\,\%: NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ $-0.77$\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$, WUE
$-0.18$\,g\,C\,mm$^{-1}$); for NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ and WUE this decline held when whole sites were resampled, when any single site was left out and
when the three sites with more than 50\,\% mortality were removed (Fig.~A11), whereas with mortality no trend remained.
A model that ignores mortality thus systematically over-estimates carbon uptake and water-use efficiency where trees
died.

\subsection*{Predictions drop where trees died}
To see how the models use mortality, we summed the SHAP values of the mortality predictors for each site (Fig.~4). These
contributions describe the fitted model, not a measured physiological response. At High sites the mortality predictors
lowered the predicted GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$ at 86\,\%, NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ at 91\,\% and WUE at 86\,\% of the sites (medians $-2.1$ and $-1.5$\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$ for
GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$ and NEP$_\mathrm{max}$), whereas at Low sites they raised the predictions slightly at 90--98\,\% of the sites (medians $+1.4$ and
$+1.2$\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$); Mid sites were mixed (58--68\,\% negative). The contributions of High and Low+Mid sites differed for all four
properties and both windows (Fig.~5; Mann--Whitney $U$ test, all $q<0.001$): at 24 months, GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$ $-2.15$ versus $+0.66$\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$,
NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ $-1.51$ versus $+0.51$\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$ and WUE $-0.24$ versus $+0.07$\,g\,C\,mm$^{-1}$ (medians). Because the sites are grouped by the
variable whose contribution is measured, this comparison shows that the learned relationship is consistent rather than
providing independent evidence; its sign and size are the informative part. Together with Fig.~3, it shows that the
models improve by lowering their predictions where trees died. Mortality accounted for 24\,\% (GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$), 28\,\% (NEP$_\mathrm{max}$),
16\,\% (ET$_\mathrm{max}$) and 23\,\% (WUE) of the mean absolute SHAP, compared with 52--66\,\% for climate and 11--20\,\% for traits.

\subsection*{The effect scales with mortality}
The contribution of mortality followed the amount of mortality closely (Fig.~6). Across sites, it declined with the site
maximum of relative mortality for GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$ (Spearman $\rho=-0.81$), NEP$_\mathrm{max}$ ($\rho=-0.83$) and WUE ($\rho=-0.78$), and less for
ET$_\mathrm{max}$ ($\rho=-0.47$; all $q<0.001$); its sign changed at about 10--20\,\% relative mortality. The error reduction did not
depend on tree cover ($\rho=0.10$ to $0.18$, not significant). The direction of the contribution was also related to tree
cover ($\rho=0.66$ to $0.75$), but less closely than to relative mortality, and it was mostly negative in shrublands and
wetlands and mostly positive in deciduous broadleaf, evergreen broadleaf and mixed forests, classes that contain few
sites (Fig.~A7). The effect of mortality on the predictions is therefore graded: the more trees died, the more the models
lowered the predicted carbon uptake and WUE.

\subsection*{The results are robust}
The results were the same with the 12-month window (Figs.~A5 and A10), with site-level tests and with
the root-mean-square error (Tables~\ref{tab:a_site} and \ref{tab:a_boot_rmse}). Because some climate variables enter the
calculation of the properties, we refitted each property without them (radiation for GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$, air temperature for ET$_\mathrm{max}$ and
WUE); $R^2$ changed by about 0.03 or less, and the error reductions, the over-prediction without mortality and its correction
remained (Table~\ref{tab:efpclim} and Fig.~A13). Finally, the five mortality metrics were strongly correlated (relative mortality
and relative deadwood, $\rho=0.91$), and relative mortality and relative deadwood carried the largest shares of the mortality
block's SHAP (26 and 24\,\%; Fig.~A9).

\section*{Discussion}
{\small\textit{Draft (about 1,000 words). Numbered citations; references in references.bib, each checked against the
paper's abstract.}}

Satellite- and aerial-based maps of tree mortality made the functional properties of woody ecosystems more predictable, and
the gain was concentrated where mortality occurred. Without mortality information, models over-predicted maximum net
ecosystem productivity, light-saturated photosynthesis and water-use efficiency in years with high mortality, and this
bias grew with the amount of mortality. Adding mortality reduced it by one- to two-thirds, depending on the learner,
and the direction of the mortality effect followed the amount of mortality rather than vegetation type or tree cover.
Mortality therefore carries information on carbon uptake that climate and plant traits do not.

The direction of the effect is consistent with what single-site studies have measured during mortality events. At a
subalpine forest hit by spruce beetles, net ecosystem exchange diminished by about half once the attacked trees died,
after an earlier phase in which hydraulic failure restricted gas exchange\cite{frank2014ecosystem}. Across North
American forests, net ecosystem production became a carbon loss after stand-replacing disturbances and returned to a
sink within 10 to 20 years\cite{amiro2010ecosystem}. Our results generalize these site observations across 101 sites
and several ecosystem types, and they suggest why mortality acts on productivity: the first and most important axis of
ecosystem function, maximum productivity, is mostly explained by vegetation structure\cite{migliavacca2021three}, and
mortality changes that structure directly. Relative mortality, which combines standing dead trees with tree-cover loss,
carried most of the information, and the direction of its effect was negative at most sites with high mortality and
slightly positive at sites with little.

The effect was not uniform, and the literature shows why. At a lodgepole pine forest where beetles killed 30 to 78\,\%
of the basal area, maximum CO$_2$ uptake did not change, because the remaining vegetation used light more efficiently
and the changes in photosynthesis and respiration cancelled out\cite{reed2014impact}. In British Columbia, beetle-killed
stands remained growing-season carbon sinks thanks to surviving trees and understory\cite{brown2009impact}, and a
synthesis of remote-sensing and flux data suggested that net ecosystem production may recover within about four years
after climate-driven mortality\cite{anderegg2016when}. Such compensation fits the shape of our results: errors changed
little at low and moderate mortality and fell sharply only in the site-years with more than 30\,\% relative mortality, and
the reduction over all site-years (11--12\,\% for maximum net ecosystem productivity) was much smaller than in
high-mortality years (29--32\,\%). Our predictors included mortality of the current and the one or two preceding years,
which allows models to capture the multi-year legacies that follow drought and mortality\cite{anderegg2015pervasive};
how quickly the functional properties recover after mortality remains to be tested with longer records.

Mortality improved water-use efficiency where it occurred but not maximum evapotranspiration. Measured responses of
evapotranspiration to tree death differ between sites: evapotranspiration fell by 28--36\,\% during a spruce beetle
outbreak\cite{frank2014ecosystem}, and evapotranspiration relative to precipitation fell by 30\,\% at a beetle-affected
site\cite{knowles2023bark}, whereas reviews of die-off ecohydrology expect the loss of transpiration and interception to
be partly offset by more evaporation from the soil and by surviving and understory vegetation\cite{adams2011ecohydrological}.
After drought-induced die-off in semi-arid basins, streamflow even decreased\cite{guardiolaclaramonte2011decreased}.
Maximum evapotranspiration, the 95th percentile of half-hourly evapotranspiration, is dominated by atmospheric demand
and is therefore less sensitive to the loss of individual trees than carbon uptake. Mortality thus appears to act more
on the carbon side of ecosystem function than on its water side.

These results matter for estimates of the forest carbon sink. Disturbances, many of them involving tree mortality,
already shape the carbon balance at large scales: a mountain pine beetle outbreak turned the forests of British Columbia
from a small carbon sink into a large source\cite{kurz2008mountain}, increasing disturbances could reduce the carbon
storage of European forests by about 500\,Tg\,C in 2021--2030\cite{seidl2014increasing}, drought and fire brought the
Siberian forest carbon sink close to neutral\cite{fan2022siberian}, and stand-replacing disturbances account for a large
part of global biomass turnover\cite{pugh2019important}. Data-driven upscaling of eddy-covariance fluxes does not include
time since disturbance or site history among its predictors, which likely contributes to biased net ecosystem exchange
patterns\cite{jung2020scaling}, and the representation of disturbance in vegetation models is a key reason why their
estimates of the northern carbon sink disagree with atmospheric inversions\cite{osullivan2024role}. Our models reproduce
this blind spot: without mortality they over-estimated carbon uptake exactly where mortality was high. With tree
mortality increasing worldwide\cite{allen2009global,hammond2022global,hartmann2022climate} and forest disturbance
regimes shifting towards younger and shorter stands\cite{mcdowell2020pervasive,seidl2017forest}, this bias is likely to
grow unless mortality is represented explicitly.

Our study has limitations. The models are predictive, so they quantify how much mortality information improves
predictions rather than the physiological mechanisms behind the change. Only 21 site-years at 10 sites had more than
30\,\% relative mortality; the strongest effects rest on these sites, although the over-prediction without mortality held
when any single site or the three most extreme sites were left out. Mortality was derived from maps around each tower
(100--500\,m buffers), which do not match the varying flux footprint exactly, and errors in the maps add noise that
probably weakens rather than strengthens the effects we report. Finally, the climate predictors cover only the period
before the growing season, and leaving out the climate variables that enter the calculation of each property did not
change the main results (Appendix~A7), so the mortality effect does not arise from circularity with the climate data.

Knowing where and when trees die makes the carbon uptake of woody ecosystems more predictable. Without that knowledge, models
systematically over-estimated carbon uptake in ecosystems with high mortality; with it, this bias shrank, and the correction
scaled with the amount of mortality regardless of vegetation type or tree cover. Mortality maps now cover large areas
at high resolution\cite{mosig2025deadtrees}, and flux networks keep growing. Adding mortality to data-driven upscaling
products and to the evaluation of land-surface models is a direct way to avoid over-estimating the carbon uptake of
woody ecosystems with high mortality.

\section*{Methods}


\textbf{Eddy-covariance data.} We used half-hourly FLUXNET data products processed with ONEFlux v1.3\cite{pastorello2020fluxnet2015},
downloaded for all available sites with the FLUXNET Shuttle on 12 May 2026. Net ecosystem exchange (NEE\_VUT\_REF) and gross
primary productivity from night-time partitioning (GPP\_NT\_VUT\_REF\cite{reichstein2005separation}) were used together with the
tower meteorology (air temperature, vapour pressure deficit, incoming shortwave radiation, precipitation) and latent heat flux.
Only measured or high-quality gap-filled half-hours (quality flags 0 and 1) entered the calculation of the ecosystem functional
properties. The 101 sites of the final dataset come from AmeriFlux (44 sites), ICOS (29), the European Fluxes Database (18),
JapanFlux\cite{ueyama2025japanflux} (3), KoFlux (2), ChinaFlux\cite{yu2006chinaflux} (2), TERN-OzFlux\cite{beringer2016ozflux,isaac2017ozflux}
(2) and FLUXNET (1); the data of each site are cited individually (Data availability).

\textbf{Ecosystem functional properties.} For each site-year we calculated four ecosystem functional properties following
Migliavacca et al.\cite{migliavacca2021three}, within the growing season, defined as the days on which daily gross primary
productivity exceeded 30\,\% of its 95th percentile (bigleaf R package\cite{knauer2018bigleaf}). Light-saturated gross
primary productivity (GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$) was obtained by fitting a light-response curve of NEE against photosynthetic photon
flux density (shortwave radiation $\times$ 2.11) in five-day windows of daytime data (shortwave radiation $>$ 50\,W\,m$^{-2}$)
and taking the 95th percentile of the fitted saturation values per year\cite{lasslop2010separation,migliavacca2021three}. Maximum
net ecosystem productivity (NEP$_\mathrm{max}$) is the 90th percentile of all half-hourly net ecosystem productivity
($-$NEE, day and night) in the growing season\cite{migliavacca2021three}. Maximum evapotranspiration (ET$_\mathrm{max}$) is the
95th percentile of half-hourly evapotranspiration (mm\,d$^{-1}$), and water-use efficiency (WUE) the median ratio of gross
primary productivity to evapotranspiration (g\,C\,mm$^{-1}$)\cite{knauer2018bigleaf}; both used daytime half-hours with shortwave radiation $>$ 200\,W\,m$^{-2}$ and
friction velocity $>$ 0.2\,m\,s$^{-1}$ and excluded half-hours within 24\,h after precipitation of more than
0.1\,mm, so that evaporation of intercepted water does not inflate evapotranspiration. Evapotranspiration was converted from latent
heat flux with the air-temperature-dependent latent heat of vaporization\cite{knauer2018bigleaf}.

\textbf{Tree mortality.} Tree mortality was derived from annual maps of standing deadwood cover and tree cover produced by
deadtrees.earth from Sentinel-2 imagery, trained on centimetre-resolution aerial imagery with manually annotated dead
trees\cite{mosig2025deadtrees} (map version 5; year 2016 excluded as unreliable). Within five nested circular buffers around each
tower (radius 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500\,m; pixels without a valid prediction masked) we calculated, per year: absolute
deadwood (deadwood cover as a percentage of buffer area), relative deadwood (deadwood cover relative to tree cover, where tree
cover $\geq$ 10\,\%, capped at 100\,\%), deadwood gain (year-on-year increase of deadwood cover, percentage points), relative
tree-cover loss (percentage of the previous year's tree cover lost) and relative mortality, (deadwood + tree-cover loss) / (tree
cover + tree-cover loss) $\times$ 100. Each metric entered the models for every buffer, for the year of the ecosystem functional
property and for the one (12-month window) or two (24-month window) preceding years. For figures, sites were classified by
their maximum relative mortality across years, averaged over the five buffers (Low $<$ mean $-$ 0.5\,s.d., High $>$ mean + 0.5\,s.d.
across sites).

\textbf{Climate and plant traits.} Climate predictors were calculated from the tower meteorology and cover only the period
before the growing season: the 12 months ending in the month before the growing-season start (lag 1) and, for the 24-month window,
the 12 months before that (lag 2). For every month we used the mean, 5th and 95th percentile of air temperature, vapour pressure
deficit and shortwave radiation, and the precipitation sum. Plant traits were 23 community-weighted mean traits at 1\,km
resolution from global maps based on crowdsourced biodiversity observations and Earth observation\cite{lusk2026crowdsourced},
averaged within 500\,m of each tower and weighted by area.

\textbf{Site selection.} Site-years were retained when all four ecosystem functional properties were available (2017--2025),
lay in runs of at least two consecutive years and had complete climate and mortality predictors including their lags, which
restricts the years to 2020--2025. Two plantation sites with extreme water-use efficiency (CN-Sb1, CN-Sb2) and site-years with
negative water-use efficiency were removed. We kept sites with a mean tree cover of at least 30\,\% within 500\,m. The final
dataset has 101 sites and 361 site-years (median 4 years per site) of evergreen needleleaf (49 sites), deciduous broadleaf (30),
mixed (7) and evergreen broadleaf (3) forests, wetlands (6), closed (3) and open shrublands (2) and woody savanna (1).

\textbf{Models.} For each property and window, two models were compared that differ only in the mortality predictors:
climate and traits (C+T; 143 predictors for 12 months, 263 for 24 months) and climate, traits and mortality (C+T+M; 193 and 338
predictors). The main learner was AutoGluon-Tabular\cite{erickson2020autogluon} (v1.6.3; default model zoo of gradient-boosted
trees, random forests, extra trees and neural networks, five-fold bagging on site-disjoint folds, weighted ensemble without
stacking). Because AutoGluon combines many model types, the results do not depend on choosing a single learner; earlier
tests with tuned XGBoost\cite{chen2016xgboost} and LightGBM\cite{ke2017lightgbm} models gave similar patterns (results not
shown).

\textbf{Cross-validation.} Models were evaluated with repeated leave-one-site-out cross-validation: for every site, models were
trained three times on a random 80\,\% of the remaining sites and the predictions for the held-out site were averaged. AutoGluon
was refitted inside every fold, so that model selection never saw the held-out site.

\textbf{Model interpretation.} Contributions of predictors were quantified with SHAP values\cite{lundberg2017unified}: computed by
permutation for the AutoGluon ensemble. The net
contribution of the mortality block is the sum of the signed SHAP values of all mortality predictors per site-year, averaged per
site; negative values mean that mortality lowers the predicted property.

\textbf{Statistics.} The main test compares the mean absolute error (MAE) of C+T+M and C+T at the site-year level: every
site-year is an observation, and the statistic is the difference of the pooled MAE (percentage change = ratio of the pooled
errors). Because the years of one site are not independent, whole sites were resampled with replacement (10\,000 bootstrap draws,
95\,\% percentile intervals) and, for a paired permutation test, the C+T and C+T+M predictions were swapped per site
(10\,000 draws, one-sided). The same tests were run for subsets of sites (High relative mortality) and of site-years (relative mortality
of the year above the Mid or High break). At the site level, per-site errors were compared with a one-sided sign-flip permutation
test on the log error ratios (100\,000 flips). The relationship between the residuals (observed $-$ predicted) and the relative
mortality of the site-year was tested with linear mixed models with a random intercept per site (nlme\cite{pinheiro2000mixed}),
checked with a site-block bootstrap of the slope (1,000 draws), leave-one-site-out refits and without the three sites with more
than 50\,\% relative mortality; the correction of the residual by mortality (residual of C+T+M minus residual of C+T) was tested per
mortality class with the paired site-level permutation (100\,000 draws). Differences in net mortality SHAP between High and
Low+Mid sites were tested with Mann--Whitney U tests and associations with Spearman correlations. $P$ values were corrected for
multiple testing with the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure\cite{benjamini1995controlling} within each table or figure. To test for
circularity, each property was refitted without the climate variables that enter its own formula (shortwave radiation for
GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$, air temperature for ET$_\mathrm{max}$ and WUE).

\textbf{Software.} Analyses used R\cite{rcore2024} (v4.3.3; nlme, bigleaf) and Python (AutoGluon, shap).

\subsection*{A7. Circularity check: EFP-specific climate block}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Concern (Miguel): a climate variable that is part of an EFP's formula could make the prediction partly circular.
        The climate predictors are already strictly pre-season, but to be safe each EFP was refitted (AutoGluon, same design)
        \textbf{without the climate variables in its formula}: GPP$_{sat}$ without radiation (light-response curve on
        PPFD = SW$_{IN}$ $\times$ 2.11), ET$_{max}$ and WUE without air temperature (ET = LE / $\lambda$(T$_a$));
        NEP$_{max}$ has no climate variable in its formula and is unchanged. Filters (SW$_{IN}$ thresholds, rain) are not
        part of the formulas and were kept. 36 (12\,m) and 72 (24\,m) climate predictors removed.
  \item \textbf{Skill hardly changes:} $R^2$ of C+T+M (12 / 24\,m) GPP$_{sat}$ 0.385 / 0.365 (before 0.367 / 0.370),
        ET$_{max}$ 0.409 / 0.420 (0.425 / 0.433), WUE 0.281 / 0.302 (0.312 / 0.326).
  \item \textbf{The mortality effect is unchanged} (Table~\ref{tab:efpclim}): WUE still improves significantly at High sites
        ($-11$ / $-15$\,\%) and in high-mortality years ($-17$ / $-20$\,\%); GPP$_{sat}$ $-4$ to $-16$\,\%, significant at
        12\,m (all site-years $-6.1$\,\%, Mid+High years $-8.3$\,\%) but not at 24\,m; ET$_{max}$ not significant.
  \item \textbf{The over-prediction without mortality and its correction remain} (Fig.~A13): in the $>$30\,\% class
        GPP$_{sat}$ is over-predicted by 4.6 and corrected by +3.6\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$ ($q=0.04$; 12\,m: +3.7,
        $q=0.009$), WUE by 0.71 and corrected by +0.25\,g\,C\,mm$^{-1}$ ($q=0.04$; 12\,m $q=0.002$); NEP$_{max}$ as before.
  \item \textbf{So:} the mortality results do not depend on climate variables that enter the EFP formulas --- no
        circularity from the climate block.
\end{itemize}
