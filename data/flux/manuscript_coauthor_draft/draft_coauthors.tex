\section*{Introduction}
    \begin{itemize}
    \item Terrestrial ecosystems regulate global \textbf{carbon, water, and energy balances}; forest functioning is closely linked to vegetation structure and functional traits.
    
    \item Increasing \textbf{drought, heat, and biotic disturbances} are causing widespread tree mortality, with potentially large consequences for ecosystem functioning.
    
    \item Mortality alters \textbf{canopy structure and leaf area}, affecting carbon uptake, evapotranspiration, and energy partitioning, with cascading effects on soils, nutrients, and vegetation recovery.
    
    \item The magnitude and trajectory of these impacts likely depend on \textbf{vegetation type and functional trait composition}.
    
    \item However, quantitative understanding of mortality impacts across ecosystems remains limited due to \textbf{spatial and temporal constraints of field observations} and limited integration of mortality and trait data.
    
    \item Advances in \textbf{remote sensing, machine learning, citizen science, and trait databases} now enable large-scale assessments of mortality and ecosystem responses.
    
    \item This creates an opportunity to quantify how mortality affects ecosystem functioning and identify \textbf{vegetation types that are more sensitive or resilient}.
\end{itemize}

        five buffers 100--500\,m, exceeds 25.0\,\% = mean + 0.5\,SD): the gain roughly doubles ---

\textbf{Figure 4: Does the direction differ between High and Low+Mid sites?} (signed SHAP)
\begin{itemize}
  \item Earlier versions of this figure used $|$SHAP$|$, which says how much the mortality block
        matters but not which way it acts. Here SHAP is recomputed keeping the \textbf{sign}: per
        site-year we sum the signed SHAP across the mortality predictors (70 for 12\,m, 105 for 24\,m) --- the block's net push
        on the prediction, in response units --- and average over the site's years.
        \textbf{Below zero means the model predicts a LOWER value of the EFP at that site} --- not
        that it predicts less accurately. Accuracy is Figure 2, where negative $\Delta$RMSE means
        \emph{less error}; here negative means \emph{lower predicted carbon uptake}. C+T+M model, natural-breaks thresholds,
        Low+Mid vs High, both Optuna learners, both windows.
  \item Classes: site maximum of the metric, mean of the five buffers; High $>$ mean + 0.5\,SD
        (total mortality: 21 High vs 83 Low+Mid; relative deadwood: 23 vs 81). Mann--Whitney U between
        the two groups, BH-FDR across the figure.
  \item \textbf{A clear dose--response.} At \textbf{High}-mortality sites the block pushes predicted
        carbon uptake \emph{down}; at \textbf{Low+Mid} sites it pushes it slightly \emph{up}
        (relative to the average site).
  \item GPP$_{sat}$ (total mortality): High $-1.76$ to $-2.32$, Low+Mid $+0.28$ to $+0.60$
        $\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$, $q<0.001$ in all four configurations.
  \item NEP$_{max}$: High $-1.35$ to $-1.59$, Low+Mid $+0.15$ to $+0.44$; WUE: High $-0.20$ to $-0.31$,
        Low+Mid $+0.11$ g\,C\,mm$^{-1}$; all $q<0.001$.
  \item \textbf{ET$_{max}$:} the shift is tiny ($-0.003$ to $-0.006$\,mm\,d$^{-1}$) but significant for total
        mortality in all four configurations; for relative deadwood only in 1 of 4.
  \item Overall 16 of 16 tests significant for total mortality, 13 of 16 for relative deadwood, and the sign
        never disagrees between learners or windows.
  \item \textbf{Read with Figure 2:} mortality improves accuracy (Figure 2) by lowering predictions at
        damaged sites. Note that sites are grouped by the same variables whose contribution is measured, so
        the test confirms that the learned relationship is consistent; the direction and size are the
        informative part.
  \item The V10 check against \emph{observed} GPP$_{sat}$ (sites pushed down have lower observed
        productivity) has not been redone for v4.1 yet.
  \item \decide{one window in the main text and the other in the supplement?}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Does it depend on ecosystem type or tree cover?}
\begin{itemize}
  \item XGBoost--Optuna, C+T+M model, both windows. \textbf{A} $\Delta$RMSE by IGBP, \textbf{B} net signed mortality
        SHAP by IGBP, \textbf{C} tree cover vs benefit, \textbf{D} tree cover vs direction,
        \textbf{E} total mortality (mean of the five buffers) vs direction. C/D/E: linear fit with a
        Spearman test per panel, BH-FDR across the board.
  \item \textbf{Panel C (benefit):} no relationship with tree cover ($\rho$ = 0.05--0.20).
  \item \textbf{Panel D (direction vs tree cover):} strong and positive for every EFP and window,
        $\rho$ = +0.46 to +0.71, all $p<0.001$ --- at low tree cover mortality pulls predicted carbon uptake
        \emph{down}, at high tree cover it pushes it \emph{up}; the linear fit crosses zero near 68\,\% cover
        (GPP$_{sat}$, 12\,m).
  \item \textbf{Panel E (direction vs mortality):} stronger still and negative, $\rho = -0.39$ to $-0.75$,
        all $p<0.001$ (GPP$_{sat}$ $-0.74$, NEP$_{max}$ $-0.73$, WUE $-0.68$, ET$_{max}$ $-0.43$ at 12\,m).
        More mortality, lower predicted carbon uptake --- the more direct version of Panel D.
  \item \textbf{Panel B by class} (median net signed SHAP, GPP$_{sat}$, 12\,m): OSH $-3.57$ ($n$=3), WET $-2.98$ (8),
        CSH $-2.35$ (3), ENF $-0.77$ (50), MF $+0.58$ (7), EBF $+0.86$ (3), DBF $+1.80$ (30).
        Unlike V10, ENF is now slightly negative.
  \item \textbf{So:} the \emph{magnitude} of the benefit does not depend on tree cover or ecosystem type; the
        \emph{direction} does, and it tracks mortality more closely than vegetation class.
  \item \textbf{Caveats.} OSH, CSH and EBF have only 3 sites each and WET 8; the correlations use all 104 sites.
  \item \decide{does this change the framing from "functional, not ecosystem-specific" to a
        direction-vs-magnitude distinction?}
  \item \decide{drop the $n\leq3$ IGBP classes from panels A and B, or keep them with a caveat?}
\end{itemize}

\section*{AutoML check: does an automated model search change the picture? (AutoGluon)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Suggested by the supervisor: instead of choosing the learner by hand, AutoGluon-Tabular trains nine model
        families (LightGBM, CatBoost, XGBoost, random forest, extra trees, two neural networks) and combines them in a
        weighted ensemble, with all choices made on site-disjoint folds. Same data, predictors, windows and
        repeated leave-one-site-out CV; AutoGluon is rerun inside every fold, so model selection never sees the
        held-out site.
  \item \textbf{Skill:} AutoGluon is best in 15 of 16 tasks; mean $R^2$ 0.471 vs 0.434 (XGBoost) and 0.438
        (LightGBM) for C+T+M, 12\,m (Table~\ref{tab:automl_r2}). The gain is largest for GPP$_{sat}$ and NEP$_{max}$
        ($\approx$+0.05); ET$_{max}$ is a tie.
  \item \textbf{What it chooses:} mainly the two neural networks (mean ensemble weight 0.48 and 0.30); tree models
        get little weight.
  \item \textbf{Same conclusion on mortality} (Fig.~6a): median per-site change in RMSE $-3.1$ to $-4.9$\,\%
        (GPP$_{sat}$), $-8.8$ to $-11.3$\,\% (NEP$_{max}$), $-0.8$ to $-1.7$\,\% (ET$_{max}$), $-2.1$ to $-5.6$\,\% (WUE);
        smaller per site than for the tree learners, although overall RMSE drops by 7--13\,\% for the carbon fluxes.
  \item \textbf{But a weaker high-mortality signal} (Fig.~6b): at the 21 high-mortality sites only NEP$_{max}$ stays
        significant for AutoGluon ($-17$ to $-18$\,\%); the tree learners capture the high-mortality sites better.
  \item \textbf{SHAP:} only permutation SHAP is possible for a neural-network ensemble (approximate, different
        baseline). It gives mortality a larger share (23--38\,\%) and traits a smaller one; its mean signed
        mortality contribution has the opposite sign for GPP$_{sat}$/NEP$_{max}$, which is most likely the
        baseline definition and still needs checking.
  \item 12 vs 24\,months: no meaningful difference for any learner.
  \item \decide{XGBoost/LightGBM as main model (exact TreeSHAP) and AutoGluon as robustness check?}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Methods}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dataset (v4.1):} deadtrees v4.1 mortality maps, FLUXNET EFPs (GPP$_{sat}$, NEP$_{max}$ following
        Migliavacca et al.\ 2021, ET$_{max}$, WUE), Lusk et al.\ 2025 trait maps (1\,km). Tree cover $\geq$30\,\%
        (500\,m, site mean), EFPs in runs of $\geq$2 consecutive years, 2016 excluded, CN-Sb1/2 removed by QC:
        \textbf{104 sites, 381 site-years}.
  \item \textbf{Models shown:} \textbf{climate + traits} vs \textbf{climate + traits + mortality} only.
        The pair differs solely by the mortality block, so it isolates its contribution.
  \item \textbf{Learners:} XGBoost and LightGBM, \textbf{both tuned with Optuna} (TPE, 50 trials per response and
        window, grouped 5-fold CV on disjoint site groups). AutoGluon-Tabular 1.6 as an AutoML check (default model
        zoo, 5-fold site-grouped bagging, weighted ensemble, no stacking).
  \item \textbf{Windows:} climate strictly before the growing season --- 12 months (lag 1) or 24 months (lag 1 + lag 2)
        ending the month before season start; mortality of the current year plus lag 1 (12\,m) or lag 1 + lag 2 (24\,m).
  \item \textbf{Cross-validation:} repeated leave-one-site-out (3 $\times$ 80\,\% of the remaining sites per held-out site).
  \item \textbf{Mortality predictors:} all metrics at all five buffers (100--500\,m). \textbf{Site classes} in the figures
        use the mean of the five buffers (site maximum across years; breaks mean $\pm$ 0.5\,SD).
  \item \textbf{Mortality metrics}: For visualisation and thresholding we define three mortality metrics, each computed within five buffers (100, 200, 300, 400, 500\,m) around the tower from the deadtrees v4.1 deadwood / tree-cover maps; the models use all buffers, the figures classify sites by the mean of the five:

\textbf{Absolute deadwood}
Standing deadwood cover as a percentage of the total buffer area — the absolute amount of dead canopy.
absolute deadwood = deadwood / area × 100 (\%)
\\
\textbf{Relative deadwood}
Deadwood relative to tree cover, what \% of the standing trees are currently dead (defined where tree cover $\geq$ 10 \%, capped at 100 \%).

relative deadwood = deadwood / tree cover × 100 (\%)
\\
\textbf{Total mortality}
Combines standing deadwood and tree-cover loss relative to forest plus loss — captures both dead-standing and removed/lost canopy.

total mortality = (deadwood + loss×100) / (forest + loss×100) × 100 (\%)
 
\end{itemize}
